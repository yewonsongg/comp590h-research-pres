========================================================================
SLIDE 01 — Agentic reasoning
========================================================================

Today, we are looking at Section 6.5 of the survey on agentic reasoning,
which focuses on autonomous web exploration and research agents.

Our central question is: what changes when a language model can act in an
environment, retain useful experience, and coordinate with other agents?

The workbench graphic introduces three capabilities: using tools and checking
observations, turning experience into reusable workflows, and combining
specialized work into a shared result.

We will follow that progression through acting, learning, and collaborating,
with closer looks at Agent Workflow Memory and Agent Laboratory.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 02 — Three capabilities. One growing system.
========================================================================

The survey organizes these systems around three complementary dimensions.

Foundational reasoning establishes the ability to plan, use tools, and
retrieve information. This is the acting layer.

[NEXT: reveal learning]

Self-evolving reasoning adds feedback and memory, allowing experience to
influence later behavior.

[NEXT: reveal collaborating]

Collective reasoning adds specialized roles and coordination. These
capabilities can coexist: a research assistant could run an experiment,
remember a debugging strategy, and ask another agent to review the result.

Let us first look at what it means for a language model to act.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 03 — From responses to real-world tasks.
========================================================================

Section 6.5.1 introduces foundational agentic reasoning. It focuses on how
we can build on traditional language models to create autonomous agents
capable of completing real-world tasks.

Traditional language models primarily generate responses based on the
information they are given. But complex tasks often require multiple steps,
external information, and interaction with different environments.

[NEXT: reveal the flight-search workflow]

For example, suppose we want a nonstop Raleigh-to-Boston flight for less
than three hundred dollars on a chosen date. A language model might suggest
an option using the information it has. An agentic system can go further:
search the route and date, filter for nonstop flights, check current fare
details and availability, and return options that meet the requirements.
This is an illustrative flight search, not a verified travel offer.

[NEXT: reveal the three foundational capabilities]

The main point is that agents need three foundational capabilities to
accomplish these tasks: planning, tool use, and search and retrieval.

Planning organizes the travel constraints and search steps. Tool use lets
the agent enter details and apply filters in a browser. Search and retrieval
find flights and bring back current fare details.

These capabilities work together: the agent can use a tool to search, and
what it retrieves may change the plan.

Together, these capabilities allow language models to move beyond generating
responses and begin executing tasks. The language model can still be the
reasoning engine within the agent.

Let us continue the same flight example to see how feedback changes a plan.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 04 — Reasoning becomes a feedback loop.
========================================================================

Imagine asking an agent to find a nonstop flight from Raleigh to Boston
for less than three hundred dollars. We have already provided the travel
date. A suitable result must meet the route, date, and budget requirements.

[NEXT: highlight Plan]

The agent makes a simple plan: enter the route and date, filter for nonstop
flights, and compare fares. This turns the goal into a few concrete steps.

[NEXT: highlight Tool action]

It opens a promising flight's fare details using the browser. Now it has
taken an action in the environment rather than just describing one.

[NEXT: highlight Observation]

The selected fare is no longer available. A promising search result is
not enough: the agent has to inspect what the environment actually returns.
This observation gives it a reason to change course.

[NEXT: highlight Updated plan]

It refreshes the results and checks another nonstop option on the same
date, within the same budget. The goal stays the same; the next action changes.

That is the feedback loop: plan, act, observe, and adjust. This is an
illustration, not a live flight search or booking. The route and budget
are example requirements, not verified offers. The same pattern applies
to web, GUI, and research agents.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 05 — Same loop. Different environments.
========================================================================

The survey applies this loop to three overlapping application families.

Web agents search, navigate, and extract information from changing online
resources. They need to select relevant evidence and decide where to look
next.

[NEXT: reveal GUI agents]

GUI stands for graphical user interface. These agents operate software by
locating elements, clicking, and typing. Grounding means connecting an
instruction to a particular target on the screen. Even a sensible plan can
fail if the agent clicks the wrong button.

[NEXT: reveal research agents]

Research agents coordinate a broader inquiry: retrieving literature,
preparing data, running experiments, interpreting results, and writing a
report.

These families overlap. A research agent might use both web browsing and
software interfaces. Across all three, the agent must check whether its
actions produced the intended result.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 06 — WebVoyager: knowing is not executing.
========================================================================

To explore one example in more detail, I looked at a paper called
WebVoyager: Building an End-to-End Web Agent with Large Multimodal Models,
published at ACL in 2024.

The researchers investigated whether a multimodal language model could
autonomously complete tasks on real websites.

WebVoyager repeats a reasoning-and-action process. It receives a task and
observes the webpage through a screenshot, with numbered interactive
elements and supporting text. It then reasons about its next action,
such as clicking a button, entering text, or scrolling.

After that action, an updated screenshot becomes the next observation.
The process continues until the agent stops or reaches its step limit.
The experiment allowed up to fifteen steps. Our drawing simplifies this
process using the flight-search example.

This combines the three foundational capabilities: planning chooses the
next steps, tool use operates the browser, and search and retrieval locate
the information needed for the task.

[NEXT: reveal the experimental result]

Across 643 tasks on fifteen real websites, the GPT-4V-based agent achieved
59.1 percent whole-task success, judged by humans. This is an absolute
success rate, not a relative improvement. It shows that autonomous browser
tasks are possible, while leaving substantial room for more reliable execution.

[NEXT: reveal the main insight]

The biggest insight is that understanding what needs to be done differs
from successfully carrying it out. The paper documents errors such as
confusing nearby elements or misreading calendar numbers as element labels.
An action can sound sensible while being applied to the wrong target.

That brings us to a concrete failure-and-recovery example.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 07 — A plan must survive contact with the page.
========================================================================

Continuing the flight search, the agent chooses a promising nonstop option.
But a cookie dialog covers the “View fare” button. It attempts a click,
and the page does not change.

If it assumes the click worked, later decisions may depend on a page state
that never existed.

[NEXT: reveal Detect the mismatch]

First, compare the expected outcome with the actual observation. Did the
fare details open?

[NEXT: reveal Repair the local action]

Second, repair the action: close the cookie dialog, locate “View fare”
again, and retry.

[NEXT: reveal Preserve the lesson]

Third, consider whether the recovery suggests a useful strategy for later
tasks.

That last step introduces self-evolving reasoning. Recovering within one
attempt is useful; retaining something that helps a future attempt adds
another capability.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 08 — Experience should change the next attempt.
========================================================================

Self-evolving reasoning does not always require retraining the model. Here,
we focus on external memory that changes the guidance available to it.

The loop begins with an attempt at a task.

[NEXT: highlight Feedback]

Feedback supplies an outcome signal: what worked, what failed, or what
remains unresolved?

[NEXT: highlight Reflection]

Reflection interprets that signal and extracts a potentially useful lesson.

[NEXT: highlight Memory]

Memory makes the lesson available beyond the current attempt. After a
successful flight search, the agent retains a procedure: enter the route
and date, apply filters, inspect fares, and check availability.

[CLICK “No memory”]

Without persistent workflow memory, a later task has no stored procedure
to consult.

[CLICK “Workflow memory”]

With memory, it can reuse those steps for a later trip with a different
route or date. It remembers how to search, while checking current fares
and availability again.

[NEXT: highlight Improved attempt]

This illustration does not measure a performance gain. It shows the
mechanism: experience becomes guidance for a later decision. That guidance
still needs to fit the current environment.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 09 — Remember a procedure, not a whole episode.
========================================================================

Agent Workflow Memory, or AWM, gives this idea a concrete method. Its problem
is that long tasks contain recurring subtasks, but agents often solve each
task without reusing what they learned.

The input is a collection of trajectories: observations and actions from
earlier tasks.

[NEXT: reveal Abstract a routine]

A language model extracts reusable subroutines. It replaces example-specific
details with variables, preserving the procedure rather than every detail
of an episode.

[NEXT: reveal Guide new actions]

The workflows become text-based memory in the action-generation prompt.
The core method provides guidance without updating the model's weights.

[NEXT: reveal the variable-based example]

A particular Raleigh-to-Boston search can become a reusable workflow with
variables for the route and date. This flight example is our teaching
adaptation of the method, not an experiment reported in the paper.

The paper's own case study extends a place-finding workflow to obtain a
postal code. It shows that a remembered routine can become a starting
point for a more complex task.

Offline AWM induces workflows from training examples. Online AWM updates
memory during a stream of tasks, using attempts that a model evaluator
predicts were successful. That evaluator can make mistakes, so memory
quality remains an important limitation.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 10 — Memory helps. Read the denominator.
========================================================================

[The Mind2Web bar appears automatically.]

The headline improvements are meaningful, but their denominators matter.

For Mind2Web, offline AWM with GPT-4 improves cross-task step success from
36.2 percent to 45.1 percent compared with MindAct. That is an increase of
8.9 percentage points, or approximately 24.6 percent relative improvement.

[NEXT: reveal WebArena]

For WebArena, online AWM reaches 35.5 percent task success, compared with
23.5 percent for the published BrowserGym baseline. That is 12 percentage
points, or approximately 51.1 percent relative improvement.

Relative improvement divides the change by the baseline. These bars do not
show absolute success rates or percentage-point increases.

There is also a metric difference. Mind2Web checks individual steps,
requiring both the correct element and action. WebArena checks whole-task
success. We should not rank the benchmarks by bar height.

The paper also reports an accessibility-tree-only BrowserGym control at
15 percent. That is a separate comparison, not the denominator for the
51.1 percent headline. These are results in specific experimental settings.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 11 — Better targeting can hide weaker actions.
========================================================================

Looking inside the Mind2Web result reveals a useful tension. With GPT-4,
element selection accuracy rises from 41.6 to 50.6 percent.

[NEXT: reveal Action F1]

However, the reported action F1 score falls from 60.6 to 57.3. F1 combines
precision and recall for the action prediction.

The authors suggest that remembered workflows can help locate a relevant
element while encouraging an action that does not fit the current state.
That is their interpretation, rather than a separately isolated causal test.

[NEXT: reveal the design lesson]

The lesson is to remember a strategy while checking whether it applies.
The agent must sometimes depart from its stored routine.

Whole-task success also remains low: 4.8 percent for AWM versus 2 percent
for MindAct on this split. Better steps do not automatically produce
reliable end-to-end completion.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 12 — Give each agent a job—and an interface.
========================================================================

Collective reasoning changes how the work is distributed. This diagram is
a conceptual coordinator-and-specialists design. A role can be a separate
model invocation with its own instructions, using the same underlying model.

[NEXT: delegate to Planner]

The planner defines subtasks, constraints, and completion conditions.

[NEXT: delegate to Researcher]

The researcher returns evidence with sources and supporting passages.

[NEXT: delegate to Tool executor]

The executor returns runnable code, settings, and measured outputs.

[NEXT: delegate to Reviewer]

The reviewer checks methods and challenges unsupported claims before the
coordinator integrates the result.

The survey describes systems with similar divisions of labor. INFOGENT,
for example, separates navigation, extraction, and aggregation.

The interface between roles matters: an agent needs to know what artifact
to return and how it will be checked. Adding agents also adds coordination
work, and several agents may still share the same errors.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 13 — A collaboration is a sequence of handoffs.
========================================================================

Consider an illustrative question: does image noise affect two model
families differently? We are tracing a workflow, not reporting new results.

[NEXT: reveal Planner]

The planner specifies a controlled comparison: the same test set, defined
noise levels, and repeated seeds where appropriate.

[NEXT: reveal Researcher]

The researcher supplies related methods and assumptions that could affect
the comparison.

[NEXT: reveal Executor]

The executor returns code, exact settings, and raw results.

[NEXT: reveal Reviewer]

The reviewer checks whether the experiment followed the protocol and
whether the conclusion matches the evidence.

If preprocessing differs between models, the coordinator should route that
issue back for correction. A vote among agents cannot repair an unfair
experiment. Explicit artifacts make these handoffs inspectable.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 14 — A research assistant, organized as a lab.
========================================================================

Agent Laboratory connects literature, experiments, and reporting around a
human-provided research idea.

Its first phase is literature review, where the PhD student agent gathers
relevant papers. These academic role names describe prompted responsibilities.

[NEXT: reveal Experimentation]

The experimentation phase includes planning, data preparation, execution,
and interpretation. The mle-solver proposes or edits programs, executes
them, scores outcomes, and reflects on errors or possible improvements.

Keeping strong candidate programs helps guide further search. However,
a high search score alone does not establish scientific validity.

[NEXT: reveal Report writing]

The paper-solver drafts and refines a report using accumulated artifacts.
Persisting code, results, and interpretation connects the phases. This also
explains why the survey discusses Agent Laboratory under memory.

[NEXT: reveal Human guidance]

In co-pilot mode, a human can provide feedback at subtask boundaries. The
result is a research report and code repository that can be inspected.

The next question is whether the system can reliably judge the quality
of what it produces.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 15 — A convincing report can fool its reviewer.
========================================================================

Agent Laboratory's autonomous evaluation generated fifteen papers: five
research questions using three model backends. Ten volunteer PhD students
each reviewed three randomly assigned papers.

[NEXT: reveal the review-score chart]

Automated reviewers gave an average overall score of 6.1 out of ten.
Human reviewers gave 3.8. That is a gap of 2.3 score points.

These are paper-review scores, not success rates or acceptance probabilities.
The finding shows why a reviewer agent should itself be evaluated. Model
approval is not automatically independent evidence of research quality.

[NEXT: reveal co-pilot results]

External reviewers gave co-pilot reports an overall score of 4.38, compared
with 3.8 for autonomous reports. Improvements were stronger in quality and
presentation than in significance or contribution.

This small, task-specific comparison does not isolate a causal effect of
human feedback. The paper also documents hallucinated experiment details
and execution failures.

The useful takeaway is that automating research assistance does not remove
the need for credible evaluation.

[NEXT SLIDE]

========================================================================
SLIDE 16 — Add capabilities. Keep the evidence loop.
========================================================================

We can now connect the three dimensions. Foundational reasoning lets an
agent interact with tools and check outcomes.

[NEXT: reveal Self-evolving]

Self-evolving reasoning adds persistent adaptation, allowing experience to
inform a later task.

[NEXT: reveal Collective]

Collective reasoning adds specialized work and coordination. This table
shows a combined design; multiple agents do not inherently require memory.

Our three takeaways are: verify what actions achieve, check what memory
preserves, and evaluate the reviewers as well as the outputs.

Acting, learning, and collaborating expand an agent's capabilities. Their
value ultimately depends on evidence that the system achieved its goal.
